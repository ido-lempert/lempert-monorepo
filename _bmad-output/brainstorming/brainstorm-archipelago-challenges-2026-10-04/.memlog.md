- (direction) Stance Creative Partner (assumed by coach, user can switch); techniques chosen by user: Analogical Thinking, then Question Storming
- (direction) Context: current pipe-drawing mechanic is boring; want challenge-based play (load spikes, race conditions, designing a new product) leading to design patterns / industry solutions; audience teens and adults; keep sim/3D world as possible base
- (technique) started Analogical Thinking
- (idea by coach) Hospital ER triage analogy: patients are requests, triage is load balancer / priority queue
- (idea by user) Analogy options: escape room (technically easiest) vs restaurant (intuitively most appealing to the user)
- (decision) User leans to restaurant as the central metaphor
- (idea by coach) Restaurant as the system: orders are requests, cooks are servers; mappings race condition=last salmon sold twice (lock), load balancer=host, cache=mise en place, queue=ticket rail, circuit breaker=86 the dish, pub/sub=kitchen display, saga=refund after burnt dish
- (decision) Player role: the owner who watches from above like a judge in a reality show
- (idea by coach) Kitchen Nightmares format: watch a failing service, pause and rewind, diagnose, then prescribe the fix, with a verdict card naming the pattern
- (idea by user) Judge sees the restaurant from above like a board game, and can dive into specific zones for detail (order tickets, dishes sent out) = architecture map plus communication mapping plus diving into logs
- (idea by user) Industry-standard technical symbols on the staff shirts and on the elements, as used in the industry
- (idea by coach) Zoom levels mirror the C4 model (context, containers, components, code/logs); order numbers on tickets work as trace IDs for following one order across stations (distributed tracing)
- (decision) Fixing the problem is by choosing a card and placing it where relevant (position matters only when relevant); moving people/equipment around is rejected as problematic
- (idea by user) At the top C4 level, offer a toggle to see the same world as a technical blueprint diagram, so players who already understand it can read the problem there and the game connects to the technical side
- (idea by coach) Dual view: the toggle is a lens, not a switch; pro players can solve from the diagram alone, learners use the restaurant to translate to the diagram (a Rosetta stone); same data, same cards
- (idea by coach) Cards have trade-offs (cost, side effects), and wrong cards are plausible, so choosing is a design decision, not a quiz; some cards need placement (where the lock goes), some are global (hire another cook)
- (decision) Card cost is three-way: service time, reputation, and money (FinOps)
- (idea by user) Couriers: 10 on duty = max service at peak but expensive, or on standby = cheap but takes time to summon, like scaling up cloud machines (balance between cost and arrival time)
- (idea by coach) Courier tiers map to cloud pricing: staff on payroll = reserved, on standby = auto-scaling with cold-start delay, freelancers = spot instances that may vanish mid-rush
- (idea by coach) Reputation is a slow variable (SLA/SLO, error budget): one bad night dents it, many good nights rebuild it; end of night shows a P&L plus reputation statement
- (decision) Both peak types: predictable peak first (capacity planning), then surprise peaks (reactive mechanisms)
- (idea by coach) Curriculum arc: predictable peak teaches capacity planning; surprise peak teaches alerting, auto-scaling and graceful degradation (shorten the menu = load shedding); peaks also expose hidden bugs like race conditions that were invisible at low load
- (technique) started Question Storming
- (insight by user) Race condition only bites when the item is the last one (stock at 1); otherwise no problem and no angry customer. When it is the last, the kitchen should raise an alert: an order came in but there are no matching raw materials (error signal for the owner)
- (question by coach) What if the kitchen alert arrives only after the waiter already promised the dish to the customer?
- (question by coach) Who receives the missing-ingredient alert: the cook, the waiter, the customer, or only the owner who dives into the zone?
- (question by coach) How does a player find a bug that only shows when stock is exactly one, if the nights before looked fine?
- (question by user) How do we keep the game always in motion and still teach, like SimCity?
- (question by coach) Is the restaurant a living, endless sim the player tends (SimCity), or a sequence of nights (episodes) with a verdict each?
- (question by coach) What plays the SimCity advisors role: a critic, a sous-chef, the customers' reviews, a tooltip on the blueprint?
- (question by coach) Can the player pause and rewind to investigate, and does time cost anything while paused?
- (question by coach) What does the player do in a quiet minute while nothing is broken?
- (question by user) At the end of the game, will the player actually know how to build and analyze software architecture?
- (question by coach) What is the final exam: read a blueprint of an unfamiliar real system and find its weak point, with no restaurant skin?
- (question by coach) Is recognising a pattern the same as knowing when NOT to use it, and where does the game teach the second?
- (question by coach) Does the player ever design from a blank board (new product) or only repair, and which of the two is the real skill?
- (question by coach) How would we measure that learning transferred to real software (a post-game blueprint challenge, a hidden pure-diagram mode)?
- (question by user) How does the player understand a card's meaning without reading hours of instructions before playing, i.e. how do we teach while playing?
- (question by coach) Can a card explain itself through a short preview of the next minute (ghost simulation) before it is played?
- (question by coach) Does the first card the player ever sees have to be obviously wrong or obviously right, so the meaning is felt rather than read?
- (question by coach) Is a card's pattern name hidden until the player has used it successfully, and is that reveal the reward?
- (question by coach) Can a failed card teach more than a successful one, and how do we make failure cheap enough to try?
- (question by user) What makes a player get hooked and invest a lot of time in the game?
- (question by user) Who is the target audience: ages, background, region, and more?
- (question by user) Does the game give value in real life, and would an organization use it for training its people?
- (insight by user) Positioning: developers increasingly generate code with AI instead of writing it, so theoretical architecture knowledge (reading, judging and directing designs) matters more than ever; this is a reason for organizations to adopt the game
- (question by coach) If AI writes the code, is the player training the skill of reviewing an AI-proposed design and spotting its weak point, and could the cards be AI proposals the player must accept or reject?
- (question by coach) Would a team lead want a shared scoreboard, a team mode where members debate a card, or reports on which concepts the team keeps failing?
- (question by coach) Is the hook mastery of a craft, a restaurant that grows and has regulars, a daily challenge like a puzzle, or competing with colleagues?
- (question by user) What is the success test for the first version?
- (question by coach) What is the smallest first slice that contains a whole restaurant night: one predictable peak, one race condition, a top-down view, one dive, three cards?
- (question by coach) Does a stranger who has never studied architecture, after ten minutes, say aloud why the last fish was sold twice and name the fix?
- (question by coach) Does a senior developer shown the blueprint lens recognise the problem in under a minute and want to keep playing?
- (question by coach) Do players replay a night to try a different card without being asked to?
- (direction) User closed the question storming and moved to converge
- (decision by coach) Convergence move 1 Affinity Clustering; clusters: (1) world and metaphor, (2) observe and investigate, (3) intervene with cards and costs, (4) structure and progression, (5) learning and real-world value; proposed through-line: see, investigate, decide, then learn the name
- (decision by user) Affinity clusters accepted; the game must not require AI at runtime (too expensive to support right now)
- (decision by coach) AI-proposal cards, if kept, are hand-authored scripted cards labelled as AI suggestions, no runtime AI; convergence move 2 MoSCoW for the first version
- (decision by user) Blueprint (technical diagram) lens stays in the Must scope of the first version
- (decision) MoSCoW v1. Must: top-down restaurant on existing sim/world, one predictable peak, last-fish race condition with kitchen alert, one dive into order tickets with order number, three cards with time/reputation/money cost, verdict card with pattern name (he+en), replay the night, read-only blueprint lens. Should: shirt symbols, card preview, standby couriers with delay, reputation across nights, investigation time. Could: surprise peak, live SimCity mode, scripted AI-suggestion cards, final exam without skin. Won't: runtime AI, team mode and org reports, full four C4 levels, other islands
- (insight by coach) Synthesis: (1) the restaurant plus the read-only blueprint lens is a Rosetta stone that serves teens, adults and org training with one dataset; (2) the hidden last-fish bug, the dive into tickets and the verdict card are one loop: notice symptom, investigate, decide, earn the pattern name; (3) a single card mechanic with a time/reputation/money cost carries load, race conditions and FinOps trade-offs (courier tiers); (4) technical risk: the current simulation has requests on a graph but no shared mutable state (stock) or time-based peaks, which a race condition needs; (5) no runtime AI; AI-suggestion cards are optional scripted content; (6) chosen direction: see, investigate, decide, then learn the name
